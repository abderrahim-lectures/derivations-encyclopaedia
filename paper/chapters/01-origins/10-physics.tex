\section[Derivations in quantum mechanics: Born, Jordan, Dirac 1925][Quantum mechanics]{Derivations in quantum mechanics: Born, Jordan, Dirac 1925}\label{sec:origins:physics}

In 1925 physicists found two facts before the algebra researchers did
\cite[p.~858]{BornJordan1925}. These facts organise much of this book. First,
the product rule still makes sense on a noncommutative algebra, if the order of
the factors is kept.
Second, on the algebra of all matrices, every map with this rule is a
commutator \cite[p.~858]{BornJordan1925}. Both facts came out of the new
matrix mechanics \cite[p.~858]{BornJordan1925}.

The first fact is in the paper \emph{Zur
Quantenmechanik}~\cite{BornJordan1925} of Born and Jordan, written in German.
In it, Born and Jordan turn the recent ideas of Heisenberg into a full theory
\cite[p.~858]{BornJordan1925}. Their mathematical tool is the calculus of
matrices~\cite[p.~858]{BornJordan1925}.

Let the entries of two matrices $a$ and $b$ be functions of a parameter $t$~\cite[(11), (11$'$), p.~861]{BornJordan1925}.
Take the derivative of each entry. This gives
\[
  \frac{d}{dt}(ab) = \dot a\, b + a\, \dot b .
\]
The same rule works for a product of $m$ factors \cite[(11), (11$'$), p.~861]{BornJordan1925}. In each term, the factors
stay in their original order~\cite[(11), (11$'$), p.~861]{BornJordan1925}.
So the derivative with respect to $t$ keeps the product rule.

Born and Jordan also use a second kind of derivative \cite[\S 2,
p.~862]{BornJordan1925}. It is the symbolic derivative $\partial y/\partial
x_k$ of a matrix function with respect to one of its matrix variables \cite[\S
2, p.~862]{BornJordan1925}. This derivative keeps neither the product rule nor
the chain rule \cite[\S 2, p.~862]{BornJordan1925}. They say so directly~\cite[\S 2, p.~862]{BornJordan1925}:
\begin{originalquote}{German}{Zum Beispiel sind hier die Produktregel der
Differentiation oder die Regel f\"ur die Differentiation einer Funktion von
einer Funktion nicht mehr allgemein in G\"ultigkeit. Nur dann, wenn alle
vorkommenden Matrizen miteinander vertauschbar sind, gelten f\"ur diese
Differentiation alle Regeln der gew\"ohnlichen Analysis.} For example, the
product rule of differentiation and the rule for differentiating a function of
a function are here no longer valid in general. Only when all the matrices
that occur commute with one another do all the rules of ordinary analysis hold
for this differentiation. \end{originalquote} \noindent So on a noncommutative
algebra, a derivative that acts on the whole algebra keeps the product rule
\cite[\S 2, p.~862]{BornJordan1925}. The derivative $d/dt$ is an example \cite[\S 2, p.~862]{BornJordan1925}. A derivative with respect to one
of the variables loses it \cite[\S 2, p.~862]{BornJordan1925}.

The second fact is due to Dirac, who asked the general question~\cite{Dirac1925}. Section~3 of his paper
is called \emph{Quantum Differentiation}. There he looks for the most
general operation $d/dv$ on quantum variables with two laws \cite[\S 3, p.~645]{Dirac1925}. In his words,
``We shall now determine the form of the most general quantum operation
$d/dv$ that satisfies the laws''
\[
  \frac{d}{dv}(x+y) = \frac{d}{dv}x + \frac{d}{dv}y,
  \qquad
  \frac{d}{dv}(xy) = \frac{d}{dv}x \cdot y + x \cdot \frac{d}{dv}y .
\]
He adds: ``Note that the order of $x$ and $y$ is preserved in the last
equation''~\cite[\S 3, p.~645]{Dirac1925}.

Quantum variables are matrices $x = (x(nm))$~\cite[\S 3, pp.~646--647]{Dirac1925}. Their rows and columns are
indexed by the stationary states \cite[\S 3, p.~645]{Dirac1925}. By the first law, each entry of $dx/dv$
is a linear combination of the entries of $x$:
\[
  dx/dv\,(nm) = \sum_{n'm'} a(nm;n'm')\,x(n'm').
\]
Dirac compares the entries on both sides of the second law, for all $x$ and
$y$ \cite[\S 3, p.~647]{Dirac1925}. Most coefficients must be zero. The rest come from a single matrix
$a$ \cite[\S 3, p.~647]{Dirac1925}.
The result is
\[
  \frac{dx}{dv} = xa - ax .
\]
So every such operation is a commutator with a fixed matrix. Dirac states
the result as follows:
\begin{quote}\small
``Thus the most general operation satisfying the laws I and II that one can
perform upon a quantum variable is that of taking the difference of its
Heisenberg products with some other quantum variable.''
\end{quote}
\noindent\cite[\S 3, pp.~646--647]{Dirac1925}.

He notes that two such derivatives do not commute in general \cite[\S 3,
p.~647]{Dirac1925}. He also notes a special case \cite[\S 3,
p.~647]{Dirac1925}. Let $a$ be the diagonal matrix of frequencies. Then the
operation becomes the ordinary derivative with respect to time~\cite[\S 3,
p.~647]{Dirac1925}.

In modern language, Dirac proved a theorem about matrix algebras \cite[\S 3,
p.~647]{Dirac1925}. Let $A = M_n(\mathbb{C})$ be the algebra of complex $n
\times n$ matrices. A linear map $D\colon A \to A$ with $D(xy) = D(x)y +
xD(y)$ is a derivation. Dirac shows that $D(x) = xa - ax$ for some $a \in A$
\cite[\S 3, p.~647]{Dirac1925}. In other words, every derivation of a full
matrix algebra is inner. His computation proves this fact in coordinates.

The same fact appears again later in the book, in two forms \cite[\S 2, p.~862]{BornJordan1925}. One form is the
vanishing of $H^1$ for separable algebras
(Section~\ref{sec:origins:cohomology}). The other form is the theorem of
Kadison and Sakai for von Neumann algebras (Chapter~\ref{ch:banach-operator}).

Dirac also remarks that $d^2x/du\,dv \neq d^2x/dv\,du$~\cite[p.~206]{Jacobson1937}. Behind this remark is
the commutator of two derivations \cite[p.~206]{Jacobson1937}. It is again a
derivation \cite[\S 3, p.~647]{Dirac1925}. Jacobson
studies this bracket in Section~\ref{sec:origins:lie-theory}
\cite[p.~206]{Jacobson1937}.

Before Jacobson, the product rule moved from matrices to polynomials over
skew fields \cite[p.~206]{Jacobson1937}. This was the work of Ore
\cite[p.~480]{Ore1933}, which we follow next.

Adler derived the electromagnetic corrections to the divergence of the
axial-vector current in perturbation theory~\cite{WlDerivationOfAdlersDivergenceCo1969}. Hagen shows that they can be
inferred
directly from the field equations, by defining the current operator anew through
an explicitly gauge-invariant limiting
procedure~\cite{WlDerivationOfAdlersDivergenceCo1969}.
