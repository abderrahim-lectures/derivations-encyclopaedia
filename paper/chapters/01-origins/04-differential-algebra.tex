\section[Differential rings and fields: Ritt and the New York school][Differential rings and fields]{Differential rings and fields: Ritt and the New York school}\label{sec:origins:differential-algebra}

Differential algebra began as an algebraic theory of systems of algebraic
differential equations \cite{Raudenbush1934}. It started with the Colloquium
volume of J.~F.~Ritt of 1932, \emph{Differential equations from the algebraic
standpoint}. Its first abstract definitions appear in a paper of Raudenbush of
1934~\cite{Raudenbush1934}. Raudenbush was a member of the school of Ritt.

Raudenbush begins with the problem \cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. He reports
two results of Ritt \cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. First, Ritt had introduced
the idea of an irreducible system of algebraic differential equations
\cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. Second, he had shown that every system is
equivalent to a finite set of irreducible systems. Raudenbush wants an
abstract ideal theory. The theorem of Ritt should then follow from
it~\cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}.

The objects of the theory are polynomials in unknowns $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ and
their derivatives \cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. Following Ritt, Raudenbush
calls them ``forms'' \cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. Their coefficients lie in
a differential field.

\begin{definition}[{\cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}}]\label{def:origins:differential-field}
A \emph{differential field} is a field $K$ with a map $\partial\colon K \to K$
such that
\[
\partial(a+b) = \partial a + \partial b, \qquad \partial(ab) = \partial(a)\, b + a\, \partial(b)
\qquad (a, b \in K).
\]
The polynomials in unknowns $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ and all their derivatives, with
coefficients in $K$, form a \emph{differential ring}, and a
\emph{differential ideal} is an ideal $I$ of it with $\partial(I) \subseteq I$.
\end{definition}

Raudenbush writes $a_1$ for the derivative of $a$
\cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. We write $\partial a$.

For abstract differential fields, Raudenbush refers to a work of R.~Baer~\cite[p.~361, footnote]{Raudenbush1934}. Its
title is \emph{Algebraische Theorie der differentiierbaren
Funktionenk\"orper}. It appeared in the proceedings of the Heidelberg
Academy~\cite[p.~361, footnote]{Raudenbush1934}.

He also records an example of Ritt \cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. In this
example a differential ideal has no finite basis in the ordinary sense
\cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. For this reason the paper works with a special
class of ideals~\cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}.

The theory that grew from here is the subject of
Chapter~\ref{ch:differential-algebra}. It includes the later work of Kolchin.

With Ritt, the first part of our story ends \cite[p.~361]{Raudenbush1934}. All
the rings in it are commutative. But in 1925 physicists had already met the
product rule on matrices, where the order of the factors matters
\cite[p.~858]{BornJordan1925}.
